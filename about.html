About this studio

The Virtual Solar Swarm is a design study: what would it take to keep a standing watch on the 200 most interesting objects in the solar system, all at once, forever? This site turns that question into working models you can pull on. It is a thing to think with, not a mission plan.

What is real here and what is not

Nothing described on this site has been built or launched, and this site does not claim otherwise. The orbital elements of the 200 targets are real published values (rounded); the physics is real but simplified; the satellites, buses, instruments, costs and timelines are design fiction grounded in current public technology. Where a page speaks of what the swarm "would" do, that conditional is doing honest work.

Every approximation, named

  • Two-body maths. Each orbit is computed as if only the Sun (or the parent planet) pulls on it. Real missions model every tug at once.
  • Coplanar circular transfers. Launch windows and fuel costs use the classic Hohmann transfer between circular orbits in one flat plane. Real windows shift with eccentricity, inclination and gravity assists, which usually help.
  • Mean-longitude window timing. Departure dates come from average orbital motion, so treat them as month-accurate at best, and rougher for very stretched orbits.
  • Stability rules of thumb. "A third of the Hill sphere prograde, half retrograde" is a widely used shorthand, not a guarantee; real stability maps are lumpy.
  • Estimated masses. Many small bodies have never been weighed. Where the catalogue says so, gravity is estimated from size and a typical density for that class of object.
  • Design lives and costs. Satellite lifetimes per environment and mass-production prices are round-number engineering guesses, made adjustable wherever they matter. Prices are shown in Australian dollars converted at 1 USD to A$1.55, an indicative August 2026 rate, with the USD figure alongside.
  • Interstellar visitors. The three visitors are shown at their catalogued paths but cannot be orbited; they are in the 200 as intercept targets.
  • Fast transfers. The push dial on the windows page models a single tangential burn at Earth's orbit, riding the resulting ellipse or hyperbola. Real fast missions add gravity assists and mid-course burns, which usually do better.
  • The gravity lab. The Lagrange landscape is the planar circular restricted three-body problem: both bodies on circular orbits, everything flattened to their plane. The classic teaching model; right for seeing why the orbits exist, not for flying them.
  • Engines. Specific impulse and cost figures for the cruise engines are round representative numbers; the Neumann Drive's mileage is its laboratory promise, marked as such wherever it appears. Thorium reactors appear only as the honest note that none has flown.
  • The census. Beyond the 200 funded targets the catalogue carries survey entries: the named moons of the giant planets and a deeper sweep of small worlds, with no dedicated satellites. It aims at every moon with a name; the unnamed provisional specks, and any names the sweep missed, wait for the next pass. Planet Nine appears clearly flagged as a hypothesis, because a design study can honestly plan for a question.
  • Convoys and the network. Convoy loads, renewal-per-window figures, light lags and trunk capacities are computed from this site's own transfer and position models, with clusters served as touring runs; real mission design would split and merge convoys far more cleverly.
  • The portraits. Every image on this site is drawn by code from the body's real class, size and known character, seeded so the same face always returns. None of them is a photograph, and none pretends to be.
  • The numbers are dials. Nothing fixes 200 targets or 1,300 satellites; they are the setting where this study started counting. The scale page solves the allocation at any fleet size with proportional laws pinned to that starting point, and holds each setting against two gates: leave the solar system unharmed, and earn its keep for humanity. Those gates are the honest version of the question "should this exist", and this site's job is to keep raising the fidelity until the question can be answered properly.

The concepts this leans on

The study assumes only technologies with flying ancestors: multi-satellite formation flying (NASA's MMS and the Starling swarm), swarm self-navigation by camera (Stanford's StarFOX experiment), deep-space laser communication (NASA's DSOC), quantum key distribution from orbit (the Micius satellite), solar observation from multiple vantage points (Solar Orbiter and the HelioSwarm concept), and satellite mass production as proven by Starlink. What has no ancestor yet is the scale, and scale is exactly what the models on this site interrogate.
