Convoys

Nothing here flies alone. Satellites ride-share: one lifter and one cruise tug carry a whole batch to a destination system, and only on arrival do they scatter to their stations. Then the drumbeat starts: every time that system's launch window comes around again, a renewal convoy rides it with fresh satellites for the ones wearing out.

A fleet of about 1,300 does not mean 1,300 launches. It means a much shorter list of well-packed convoys, and the solar system's own timetable, from the windows model, decides when each one can leave.

Renewal need is the average number of replacement satellites that come due between one window and the next, from each environment's design life. Clusters like the main belt are served as touring convoys that drop satellites at several neighbours on one run; deep clusters renew on generational timetables. Interstellar interceptors are not convoyed; they wait fuelled near Earth.

How a convoy works

1 · Pack

A heavy lifter climbs the well with a cruise tug and a rack of satellites, folded like chairs. The rides page holds both machines.

2 · Cruise

One tug does the interplanetary work for the whole rack, then brakes the stack into a wide capture orbit at the destination, the same move you can play by hand in the gravity lab.

3 · Disperse

From the wide orbit, each satellite spends a few hundred metres per second of its own delta-v drifting down to its station: moons, ledges and pickets. The tug either returns for reuse or retires as a comms relay for the network.
