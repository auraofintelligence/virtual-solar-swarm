The control ladder

1 · Reflex

Milliseconds, onboard. The satellite's own edge AI dodges debris, points instruments at a sudden plume, keeps itself alive. Nobody is asked.

2 · Swarm vote

Seconds to minutes, over the local radio mesh. The picket votes on a new formation or a chase, the way the flown Starling swarm demonstrated. Consensus, not command.

3 · System plan

Hours, at the system relay hub. Weekly observing plans, convoy arrivals, cross-picket coordination for a whole planetary system.

4 · Policy

Days, from Earth. Humans set priorities and budgets, never joystick a spacecraft. Commands travel signed, with keys exchanged on the tamper-proof quantum channel.

The relay architecture

Trunk and side doors

The main road is laser: tight beams from each system hub to Earth's receiving telescopes, the technology the DSOC experiment proved across hundreds of millions of kilometres. But once each synodic year most systems pass behind the Sun, and the beam is blown out by our own star. The fix is geometry: relay stations riding 60° ahead of and behind Earth, on the same L4 and L5 ledges you can stand on in the gravity lab, so there is always a bent path around the glare. Store and forward

When no path is open at all, nothing is lost. Every satellite and hub holds its mail and passes it on when a link returns: delay-tolerant networking, the interplanetary internet model proven on the space station. Retired convoy tugs take up posts as extra letterboxes. The network is slower than Earth's, never broken.
